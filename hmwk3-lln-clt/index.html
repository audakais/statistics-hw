n systems, m attackers: the penetration score as n grows

A sum of i.i.d. scores, simulated step by step for many attackers, and looked at with three different scalings: Sn, Sn/n and Sn/√n. One of them spreads out, one concentrates on a single number (law of large numbers), and one keeps a fixed shape that turns into a normal curve (central limit theorem).

01The model

There are n systems and m attackers. Each attacker goes through the systems one after the other, and step i of an attacker's trajectory is the score Xi obtained on system i. All the Xi are independent and identically distributed: within one attacker (system i does not affect system i+1) and across attackers (they work independently of each other). So the m attackers are m independent copies of the same random trajectory, and their final scores are a sample of size m from the distribution of the score.

  • Bernoulli model. Xi = 1 if the attacker penetrates system i, 0 otherwise, with P(Xi = 1) = p. Then Sn is the number of systems the attacker got into, and Sn/n is the fraction of them, the attacker's success rate.
  • Rademacher model. Xi = +1 (the attacker gains ground) or −1 (the defence wins and the attacker loses ground), each with probability 1/2. Sn is the net score after n systems, i.e. a simple symmetric random walk. It describes a fair contest where neither side has an edge, so the mean is exactly zero.

With μ = E[Xi] and σ² = Var(Xi), independence gives E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ². Dividing by a constant c divides the variance by c², so the three quantities in the assignment behave like this:

What the theorems say. The weak law of large numbers says that Sn/n → μ in probability: for every ε > 0, P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0. With a finite variance this follows directly from Chebyshev's inequality:

P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) ≤ Var(Sn/n) / ε² = σ² / (nε²) → 0

In the Bernoulli case that is exactly the statement on the board: the probability mass of the average score concentrates on p = P(Xi = 1) as n → ∞. The central limit theorem says that (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) converges in distribution to N(0, 1). In the Rademacher case μ = 0 and σ = 1, so this is Sn/√n itself. In the Bernoulli case Sn/√n must first be centred and rescaled, because its mean p√n runs off to infinity.

simulation settings

Every attacker is simulated step by step up to n = 10 000 systems. The value of Sn is stored at n = 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, …, 10 000, so all the values of n come from the same trajectories, just like a real attacker's score evolves. The random generator is seeded (11 for Bernoulli, 22 for Rademacher), so the default run is always the same, and node tools/experiments.js prints the same numbers from the command line.

Choosing m. The attackers are the Monte Carlo sample, so m sets how precisely the distributions can be seen, not how they behave. A probability q estimated from m attackers has standard error √(q(1−q)/m), which is at most 0.011 for m = 2000. A sample variance is off by roughly √(2/m) ≈ 3% in relative terms. The distance between an empirical CDF and the true one is about 0.87/√m ≈ 0.02 even when the model is exactly right. 200 attackers already show the trends; 2000 is enough to read the numbers below with two significant digits.

02Sample trajectories

The first 40 attackers, plotted as a function of the number k of systems visited so far. The shaded band is mean ± 2 standard deviations from the formulas in section 01. Switching between the three quantities shows the same paths with a different scaling.

  • Sk. In the Bernoulli model the paths climb along the line pk and fan out, because the band has width proportional to √k. In the Rademacher model they wander around 0 and the fan opens up in both directions: a fair game does not keep a single attacker near zero.
  • Sk/k. The same paths squeezed towards p (or 0). They jump around a lot for the first few systems and then settle, since each new system changes the average by at most 1/k. This is the law of large numbers for one trajectory at a time.
  • Sk/√k. For Rademacher the band stays at ±2 for every k. The paths neither settle nor blow up, they keep moving inside a band of constant width. For Bernoulli the whole bundle drifts up like p√k, which is why it has to be centred before the CLT applies.

  • The simulation follows the exact curve. The differences are of the size expected from m attackers. With the default settings (p = 0.3, ε = 0.05) the share of Bernoulli attackers outside p ± 0.05 goes from 0.71 at n = 10 to 0.099 at n = 200, 0.0005 at n = 1000 (one attacker out of 2000), and none from n = 2000 on.
  • Chebyshev is valid but loose. It is always above the true probability, but by a large factor: at n = 1000 it gives 0.084 for Bernoulli, while the exact value is 0.0005. It only uses the variance. The real tail decays exponentially in n, while the bound only decays like 1/n. For the LLN that does not matter, since 1/n already goes to zero.
  • The curve is not monotone for small n. Sn/n can only take the values k/n (Bernoulli) or (2k − n)/n (Rademacher). For Rademacher with an odd n the average can never be 0, so for n = 1 and n = 5 every attacker is more than 0.05 away from 0 (probability 1). For n = 2 half of them land exactly on 0. This lattice effect disappears as the grid of possible values becomes finer than ε.
  • The symmetric case converges more slowly. With the same ε, Rademacher needs a larger n than Bernoulli with p = 0.3, because its variance is 1 instead of 0.21. The probability depends on nε²/σ², so a variance about 5 times larger needs about 5 times more systems for the same precision.

√n is the only scaling that keeps the variance fixed. Divide by less (Sn itself) and the spread grows without limit. Divide by more (Sn/n) and it shrinks to zero, so the distribution collapses onto a single point and there is no shape left to see. That is why the LLN is a statement about Sn/n (where the limit is a constant) and the CLT is a statement about Sn/√n (where the limit is still random). The two are consistent: Sn/n = (Sn/√n)/√n, a quantity of fixed size divided by something that goes to infinity.

  • From two spikes to a bell. For n = 1, S1 is just ±1, two bars and nothing normal about it. By n = 20 the outline already follows the bell curve, and from n = 100 on what is left of the gap comes mostly from the lattice and from the finite m (next two points). The variance was 1 all along (section 05). What changes with n is the shape.
  • D stops decreasing at about 0.02. This is not a failure of the CLT. With m = 2000 attackers even a sample drawn from an exact N(0, 1) would give D of about 0.87/√2000 ≈ 0.019, so past n ≈ 1000 the remaining distance is Monte Carlo noise. Increasing m lowers this floor.
  • Sn lives on a lattice. Sn/√n takes values spaced 2/√n apart, so the true CDF is a staircase and can never match Φ exactly. Each step has height about 0.8/√n, and for n = 10 000 that is below 0.01. The CLT is about convergence of the CDFs at every point, not about turning a discrete variable into a continuous one.
  • Bernoulli is skewed for small n. With p = 0.3 the standardised score has a longer right tail (third moment (1−2p)/√(np(1−p)) > 0), so it starts further from the normal than the symmetric Rademacher case at the same n. It catches up as n grows. The Rademacher increments are symmetric, so this skewness term is zero for them.

07What it means for n systems and m attackers

effect of the number of systems n

  • The total grows, and so does its uncertainty, but more slowly. An attacker's expected number of penetrated systems is np and the typical deviation from it is √(np(1−p)). The absolute uncertainty grows like √n, while the relative one, √((1−p)/(np)), shrinks like 1/√n. With p = 0.3 and 10 000 systems, an attacker gets into about 3000 ± 46 of them.
  • The success rate becomes predictable. On a small network (n = 10) two attackers with the same skill p can look very different, one getting into 1 system and another into 5. On a large one their success rates are almost identical and equal to p. For a defender this is good news and bad news: the fraction of a large fleet that will fall is easy to predict, but it does not get smaller as the fleet grows. Only lowering p does that.
  • A fair contest still produces big individual swings. In the Rademacher model the average score goes to 0, so in the long run neither side wins per system. But the net score of a single attacker is typically of size √n (E|Sn| ≈ √(2n/π), about 80 for n = 10 000). Some attackers end far ahead and some far behind, with no difference in skill.

effect of the number of attackers m

  • m does not change any single attacker's score. The distribution of Sn for one attacker is the same whether there are 10 attackers or 10 000. What m changes is how well the sample shows that distribution (section 01) and the quantities that combine attackers.
  • The aggregate score. The total over all attackers, T = Sn(1) + … + Sn(m), is a sum of nm i.i.d. increments, so E[T] = nmμ and Var(T) = nmσ². Its average T/(nm) obeys the LLN with nm in place of n. Adding attackers works just like adding systems.
  • The best attacker. The maximum over m attackers grows with m. The table below takes the first m′ attackers of the simulation and reports the highest success rate among them. For small n the best of many attackers looks much stronger than p, purely by luck. As n grows the maximum is pulled down towards p too, because every attacker's average converges.
  • Systems hit by at least one attacker. A defender cares about which systems fall, not who gets in. If the m attackers act independently on the same system, it survives with probability (1−p)m, so the share of compromised systems tends to 1 − (1−p)m (again by the LLN, this time across systems). This grows quickly with m: with p = 0.3, two attackers already compromise about half the network and ten compromise about 97%.

limits of the model

All of this relies on the i.i.d. assumption. Real systems differ (each has its own pi), real attackers learn from one system to the next (the increments become dependent), and defenders react after the first intrusions. With different but independent pi the LLN still holds in the form Sn/n − mean(pi) → 0 and a version of the CLT (Lindeberg's) still applies as long as the total variance p1(1−p1) + … + pn(1−pn) grows to infinity, for example when the pi stay away from 0 and 1. With strong dependence, for example one stolen password that opens many systems at once, the variance of Sn can grow faster than n and the √n picture no longer holds.
