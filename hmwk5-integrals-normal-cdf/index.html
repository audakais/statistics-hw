Riemann vs. Lebesgue sums, and computing the normal CDF

The first part computes the same integrals by cutting the domain (Riemann) and by cutting the range (Lebesgue), and compares accuracy, convergence and cost, including two pathological functions. The second part explains why Φ(x) has no elementary formula, how the error function fixes that on paper, and which numerical method actually gives its digits.

Exercise 1 · Riemann and Lebesgue integration

01Two ways to cut the area

Riemann. Split [a, b] into N subintervals of width h = (b − a)/N, pick a tag ξk in each one and add up the rectangles. I use two tags: the right endpoint (the plain definition) and the midpoint (the usual refinement).

RN = h · Σk=1..N f(ξk) ξk = xk (right) or (xk−1 + xk)/2 (midpoint)

Lebesgue. Split the range of f instead, with levels lo = t0 < t1 < … < tN, and take the preimages Ek = {x : tk ≤ f(x) < tk+1}. The simple function equal to tk on Ek lies below f, and its integral is Σ tk μ(Ek), where μ is length. Writing m(y) = μ{x : f(x) ≥ y}, so that μ(Ek) = m(tk) − m(tk+1), and summing by parts:

LN = Σk tk μ(Ek) = t0(b − a) + Σk=1..N (tk − tk−1) · m(tk)

This is the lower Lebesgue sum. It is always ≤ the integral, and it increases to it as the levels get finer (monotone convergence). Its limit is the "layer cake" formula ∫f = lo·(b − a) + ∫lohi m(y) dy. As the Riemann refinement, I also evaluate m in the middle of each band, (tk−1 + tk)/2.

The algebra shows what each method really is numerically. A Riemann sum is a quadrature of f(x) in x. A Lebesgue sum is a quadrature of m(y) in y. So each one is accurate when its own integrand is smooth, and that is what explains every result below. The price of the Lebesgue side is that m(y) must be known: for each level I need the measure of a preimage, which means inverting f.

Riemann fills vertical strips, one per subinterval. Lebesgue fills, at every x, up to the highest level that f reaches there, so the shaded region is made of horizontal bands over the preimages.

02The functions and their preimages

I used the examples of the assignment and added the standard normal density on [0, 3], which links this exercise to the next one. The exact values are 2, 1/3, 2, 1/4, 2, 1/2, Si(π) and Φ(3) − 1/2. Si(π) = ∫0π sin(x)/x dx has no elementary form either, so I computed it from the series Σ (−1)nπ2n+1/((2n+1)(2n+1)!) and Φ(3) with the reference method of Exercise 2. For 1/√x, which is unbounded, the levels are geometric, tk = Yk/N with Y = N². The part above Y is left out, and it is worth exactly 1/Y.

For seven of the eight functions the preimage has a closed form. For sin(x)/x it does not: the function is decreasing on [0, π], so {f ≥ y} is an interval [0, r(y)], but r(y) solves sin(r)/r = y, and I find it by bisection with 60 steps.

03Accuracy and convergence

I used N = 3, 7, 15, …, 16383 (that is, 2j − 1). An odd N is deliberate: with an even N the point 0.5 is always a grid node, and the jumps and the kink at 0.5 would be integrated exactly by luck.   • Both approaches converge to the same value for every function here. All eight are Riemann integrable (1/√x as an improper integral), and the theorem says the Lebesgue integral then exists and is equal. The numbers agree with it: every column goes to zero.
  • The plain versions are both first order. The right-endpoint Riemann sum and the lower Lebesgue sum have slope −1 for the smooth functions. For sin x and |x − 0.5| the right Riemann sum is second order, because the function takes the same value at both ends of the interval. Then the right-endpoint sum equals the trapezoid rule.
  • Smooth functions favour Riemann. The midpoint rule has slope −2. The middle-level Lebesgue sum only reaches about −1.5 for sin x, x² and sin(x)/x. The reason is that each of them has a flat point (f′ = 0 at π/2, at 0 and at 0), and near the corresponding level the preimage changes very fast: for sin x, m(y) = π − 2 arcsin y has infinite slope at y = 1. A quadrature of a function with a square-root singularity loses half an order, which is what the table shows. For the normal density the flat point is at x = 0. Its Lebesgue error does not settle into a clean power law in this range, and the fitted slope is −1.28.
  • Step functions favour Lebesgue. For both step functions m(y) is constant inside the range (m = 0.5), so both Lebesgue sums are exact for every N. Riemann always has one cell that straddles the jump, and the error there is (size of the jump) × h/2. It is first order even with midpoints. The same happens with |x − 0.5|: m(y) = 1 − 2y is linear, so the middle-level sum is exact. The midpoint Riemann sum loses h²/4 in the cell that contains the kink.
  • The singular function favours Lebesgue by far. For 1/√x the midpoint Riemann error only decreases like N−1/2 (9.5·10−3 at N = 4095), because the cells near 0 miss a large part of the area. In the range, m(y) = 1/y² is perfectly smooth. With geometric levels the middle-level Lebesgue error is 4.2·10−6 at the same N, more than 2000 times smaller. To be fair, a Riemann sum on a graded grid, refined near 0, would also recover. The point is that the Lebesgue partition adapts to the singularity without being told where it is.

04Computational cost

Both sums cost N "units", but the unit is different: one evaluation of f for Riemann, one evaluation of m for Lebesgue. Times are measured live in this browser, N = 4095, averaged over repeated runs.

  • When m has a closed form, the two methods cost about the same, with Lebesgue at most two or three times slower. The Lebesgue loop also has to compute the levels, and asin or sqrt costs about as much as sin.
  • When m has to be found numerically, the cost is multiplied by the cost of the inversion. For sin(x)/x every level needs 60 evaluations of f for the bisection, and the Lebesgue sum becomes 30 to 50 times slower in my runs, for a less accurate result.
  • For a function of several variables or with many oscillations, there is no closed form and no simple bisection. The only general way to obtain μ(Ek) is to sample f on a fine grid and count the points falling in each band. The table below does this for sin x with N = 1000 levels and M sample points.

The counted version stops improving at about 10−5, because each sample is rounded to the middle of its band. With the same M samples the midpoint Riemann sum reaches 10−10. Once the measure is obtained by counting grid points, the Lebesgue sum is simply the Riemann sum regrouped by value, plus the rounding to the levels. So as a numerical method, Lebesgue is only competitive when the preimages are cheap to measure. Its real strength is as a definition, as the next section shows.

05Pathological functions

Dirichlet function, f = 1 on the rationals and 0 on the irrationals of [0, 1]. Every subinterval contains both kinds of points, so every upper Darboux sum is 1 and every lower one is 0, for any partition. The Riemann sums depend only on the choice of tags, and the function is not Riemann integrable. On the Lebesgue side the partition of the range only has to separate 0 and 1. The preimage of 1 is ℚ ∩ [0, 1], which is countable. Covering its k-th element with an interval of length ε/2k shows that its measure is below ε for every ε, so it is 0. Hence ∫f = 1·0 + 0·1 = 0, exactly, with a single level.

Every double is a fraction m/2j, so a program that evaluates the Dirichlet function on floating-point inputs, even random ones, finds 1 everywhere and returns 1. This function cannot be integrated by sampling at all. Only the measure-theoretic argument gives the answer.

Thomae's function, f(p/q) = 1/q in lowest terms and 0 on the irrationals. It is discontinuous at every rational, but only finitely many points have f > ε, so it is Riemann integrable, with integral 0. The lower Darboux sums are all 0. The upper sum takes, on each cell, 1/q for the smallest denominator q of a fraction inside the cell. I computed it, and the Riemann sums with rational tags, exactly in integer arithmetic.

All Riemann-type values go to 0, but slowly: the upper sum only drops by a factor of about 3.5 for every factor 10 in N, roughly like N−0.55, and the right-endpoint sum is still 1.3·10−3 at N = 104. The Lebesgue integral is 0 at once: f is nonzero only on a set of measure zero, so it equals 0 almost everywhere. The last column evaluates the function on the doubles nearest to the midpoints. It gives exactly 0.5/N, a value near 0, but for a meaningless reason. Almost all those doubles have a denominator of 253 or more, and the sum comes only from the few midpoints that are exact binary fractions: 1/4 and 3/4 for N = 10, sixteen odd multiples of 1/32 for N = 104.

What the two examples show. When a function is Riemann integrable, the Lebesgue integral gives the same number (section 03, and Thomae's function here). When it is not, as with Dirichlet, the Lebesgue integral can still exist and be obvious. The improper case 1/√x is in between: Riemann needs a limit, Lebesgue handles it directly, and numerically the range partition is much better.

Exercise 2 · Numerical computation of the normal CDF

06Why Φ has no elementary formula

Φ(x) = P(Z ≤ x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt, φ(t) = e−t²/2 / √(2π)

φ is continuous, so by the fundamental theorem of calculus Φ is an antiderivative of φ. An antiderivative therefore exists. The course notes on Hogg, McKean and Craig §3.4 make exactly this correction to the textbook's wording. What fails is that this antiderivative cannot be written with elementary functions, meaning functions built from rational functions with finitely many arithmetic operations, compositions, exponentials, logarithms and algebraic functions (sin, cos and arctan are included, since they are complex exponentials and logarithms).

Liouville's theorem, in the form given by Rosenlicht (Integration in finite terms, Amer. Math. Monthly 79, 1972), which the notes cite. Let f and g be rational functions with g not constant. Then ∫ f(x) eg(x) dx is elementary if and only if there is a rational function R with

R′(x) + g′(x) R(x) = f(x), and then ∫ f eg dx = R eg + C

For the normal density f = 1 and g = −x²/2, so I need a rational R with R′ − xR = 1. There is none:

  • R has no poles. Suppose R has a pole of order k ≥ 1 at a point a (possibly complex). Then R′ has a pole of order k + 1 at a, while xR has a pole of order at most k. The two cannot cancel, so R′ − xR has a pole at a, but it should equal 1, which has none.
  • So R is a polynomial, and degrees do not match. R = 0 gives 0 ≠ 1. If R has degree d ≥ 0, then xR has degree d + 1 and R′ has degree d − 1. So R′ − xR has degree d + 1 ≥ 1 and cannot be the constant 1.

Hence ∫ e−x²/2 dx is not elementary. Neither is Φ: if it were, √(2π)Φ(x) would be an elementary antiderivative of e−x²/2. The same argument with g = −x² covers e−x² and the error function. Note that the theorem does not say that Φ is hard to compute. It only says that no finite formula with the usual functions gives it.

07The error function: exact on paper, numerical in practice

Since the integral cannot be expressed with the known functions, the standard move is to give it a name and study it as a new function:

erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫0z e−u² du, erfc(z) = 1 − erf(z)

In Φ(x) = 1/2 + (1/√(2π)) ∫0x e−t²/2 dt, substitute t = √2·u, dt = √2·du. The upper limit becomes x/√2 and the constant √2/√(2π) = 1/√π, so:

Φ(x) = ½ [ 1 + erf(x/√2) ] 1 − Φ(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2)

This representation is exact. erf is a well-understood function: entire, odd, with erf(∞) = 1 and with a known power series, continued fraction and asymptotic expansion. Every property of Φ can be derived from those of erf. But a name is not a number. To get the digits of erf(1.3), one still has to sum a series or evaluate an approximation. The same is true of exp and sin: they are only "known" because a library evaluates them with polynomial approximations. The difference is practical. exp and sin are built into every language, while erf often is not (JavaScript has no Math.erf), so the method has to be chosen and implemented.

There is also a precision trap. In double precision, 1 − Φ(x) for large x cannot be obtained as 1 − Φ(x): the spacing of doubles just below 1 is 1.1·10−16, so Φ(8) = 1 − 6.2·10−16 keeps only one digit of its tail, and from about x = 8.3 on Φ(x) rounds to exactly 1. A good method must compute the small one of Φ(x) and 1 − Φ(x) directly, which is the job of erfc.

08Five methods, and the one I would use

By symmetry, Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x), so every method only needs x ≥ 0.

The positive series is the Taylor series multiplied through by ex²/2. Its terms are x2k+1/(1·3·…·(2k+1)), which can be checked by differentiating: the derivative of φ(x)S(x) must be φ(x), which gives S′ = 1 + xS, and the series satisfies it term by term. I evaluate the continued fraction forward with the modified Lentz algorithm, stopping when the update factor equals 1 to machine precision, so the number of terms adapts to x.

Final method. The results in the next section show that the series and the continued fraction are complementary. So for |x| < 2 I use the series, which needs at most 24 terms. For |x| ≥ 2 I use the continued fraction, which needs at most 106 terms and only 12 at x = 10. In both cases it returns Φ(x) and 1 − Φ(x) separately. 

function normalCdf(x) {
  const ax = Math.abs(x);
  let upper;                                      // P(Z > |x|)
  if (ax < 2) upper = 0.5 - pdf(ax) * seriesSum(ax);
  else upper = pdf(ax) / cfDenominator(ax);
  return x >= 0 ? { p: 1 - upper, q: upper } : { p: upper, q: 1 - upper };
}

One detail matters in the far tail: φ(x) = e−x²/2/√(2π), and the rounding error of x·x is multiplied by x²/2 inside the exponential. I split x² = xh² + (x − xh)(x + xh), with xh a multiple of 1/16. Then xh² is exact and the remaining part is small. The other files are in the page source: normcdf.js contains all the methods.

To measure errors around 10−16, the reference must be much more precise than a double. I wrote it with integer (BigInt) arithmetic in fixed point, with 80 + 0.434·x² decimal digits. 10Accuracy and efficiency

Each chart shows, for x from −8 to 8 in steps of 0.05, the relative error of the smaller of Φ(x) and 1 − Φ(x). This is the value one needs for p-values and tail probabilities. For the methods that compute only Φ, the tail is taken as 1 − Φ. The dashed line marks the double-precision limit 1.1·10−16.

  • Simpson with 64 subintervals has an absolute error of at most 2.2·10−9. Its convergence is the expected h4: at x = 2 the error drops by 16 each time n doubles, from 4.9·10−5 with n = 4 to 2.2·10−12 with n = 256. The trapezoid rule is h². Being an integral from 0, it gives the tail as ½ minus something close to ½, so the relative error in the tail grows without limit. It is also the slowest method, with 65 exponentials per value.
  • The alternating Taylor series is excellent up to |x| ≈ 3 and then collapses: the tail error is 10−10 at x = 4, 10−6 at x = 5 and 6% at x = 6. Its terms reach about 4·105 at x = 6 before cancelling down to a result below 1, and every digit lost in the cancellation is lost for good. At x = 8 even Φ has an absolute error of 3·10−5. This is the method suggested in the course notes. On paper it is correct for every x, and it is reliable only for small x.
  • The positive series gives Φ to about 10−15 everywhere, because nothing cancels inside the sum. But for large x the tail is still ½ − (something close to ½), and the relative error there grows like the size of 1/(1 − Φ).
  • The continued fraction is the opposite: in the tails it needs few terms (106 at x = 2, 52 at x = 3, 12 at x = 10) and gets the tail to full relative precision, even at x = 20 where it is 10−89. But at x = 0.5 it needs 1564 terms, and for |x| ≤ 0.2 it does not converge within my 5000-term limit.
  • Abramowitz & Stegun is the fastest and meets its stated bound: the largest absolute error I found is 7.41·10−8 (bound 7.5·10−8). Seven digits are fine for a printed table, but in the tail the error is relative to a tiny number. At x = 8 it is 7%.
  • The final method takes the good half of the two series methods. The absolute error on Φ is at most 2.5·10−16 and the relative error on the tail is at most 7.8·10−15, at x = −1.85. The remaining error comes from the subtraction ½ − φS just below the switch, where the tail is still about 0.03. In my runs (Node 24, on 106 random points in [−6, 6]), A&S took about 0.13 μs, the series 0.3 μs, the final method 0.7 μs and Simpson 4.4 μs. The continued fraction alone averaged 8 μs, because of the points near 0. The high-precision reference takes about 0.27 ms. Your browser will give different absolute numbers, but the order should be the same.

Conclusion. Being expressed with erf does not settle how to compute Φ. Each method is accurate somewhere and poor elsewhere. Numerical integration is general but slow, and it loses the tail. The Taylor series of the notes is ruined by cancellation beyond |x| ≈ 3, and polynomial fits stop at their design precision. Splitting the line, with a series in the centre and a continued fraction in the tails, gives essentially full double precision for both Φ and 1 − Φ at under a microsecond per value. This is the same structure used by the standard library routines, which split the real line into ranges and use a different expansion on each.
