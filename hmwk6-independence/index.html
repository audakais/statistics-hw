Statistical independence: contingency tables and the chi-square test

Starting from raw records, the page builds the contingency table of two categorical variables, its marginal and conditional frequencies, the table that independence would predict, and the chi-square statistic with its p-value. The data are the 1973 Berkeley graduate admissions, a real dataset where the answer to "are these two variables independent?" changes once a third variable is taken into account, which makes it a good case for separating association from causation.

01The dataset

In fall 1973 the University of California, Berkeley received 4526 applications to its six largest graduate departments. Each application has three categorical attributes: the applicant's Gender (Female, Male), the outcome Admit (Admitted, Rejected) and the department Dept, anonymised as A to F. The figures were published by Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, "Sex Bias in Graduate Admissions: Data from Berkeley", Science 187 (1975), 398–404, and are distributed with R as datasets::UCBAdmissions. I downloaded the CSV version from the Rdatasets collection (vincentarelbundock.github.io/Rdatasets) and checked that its totals add up to 4526.

The file is already aggregated: 24 lines, one per combination, with a frequency column. To do the exercise "programmatically" from the bottom, I first expand it into one record per applicant and shuffle the records (seeded, so the order is reproducible). From that point on the code only sees a list of 4526 individual observations, as it would with any raw bivariate dataset, and every table on this page is recomputed from it.

The analysis below works on any pair of the three variables, on all departments or on one. The default is the question that made the data famous: is the admission outcome independent of gender?

02Contingency table, marginal and conditional frequencies

Let X take the values x1, …, xI and Y the values y1, …, yJ. The contingency table counts nij, the number of records with X = xi and Y = yj. The code makes one pass over the records: it collects the distinct values of each variable, gives each value an index, and adds 1 to cell (i, j) for every record. The row and column totals are the marginal counts ni· = Σj nij and n·j = Σi nij, and N is the grand total.

joint fij = nij / N marginal fi· = ni· / N, f·j = n·j / N conditional f(yj | xi) = nij / ni· (row profile), f(xi | yj) = nij / n·j (column profile)

For Gender × Admit the row profiles already tell most of the story: 44.5% of the men and 30.4% of the women were admitted, while the marginal admission rate is 1755/4526 = 38.8%. If the outcome did not depend on gender, both rows would show (up to noise) that same 38.8%. The column profiles say the same thing the other way round: women are 40.5% of the applicants but only 31.7% of the admitted.

One panel per value of X. Each bar is a conditional frequency f(y | x); the dashed mark above or below it is the marginal f(y). Under independence every bar would reach its mark.

03Expected frequencies under independence

If X and Y were independent, the joint frequency of every cell would be the product of the two marginal frequencies, fij = fi· f·j. Multiplying by N gives the count that each cell would contain if the margins stayed as they are and the association were removed:

Eij = N · fi· · f·j = ni· · n·j / N

The expected table has exactly the same row and column totals as the observed one, so the comparison is only about how the counts are spread inside the table. Equivalently, every row of E has the same profile, the marginal f·j. For Gender × Admit, E puts 711.5 admitted women in the table against the 557 observed, about 154 fewer admissions for women (and 154 more for men) than independence predicts.

To see which cells drive the result I use the adjusted residual (nij − Eij) / √(Eij(1 − fi·)(1 − f·j)), which is approximately standard normal under independence; values beyond ±1.96 are highlighted. For a 2 × 2 table the four adjusted residuals have the same absolute value (here 9.60), because there is only one free cell once the margins are fixed, and its square is the chi-square statistic.

04Chi-square statistic and p-value

Pearson's statistic adds the squared differences between observed and expected counts, each scaled by the expected count:

χ² = Σi Σj (nij − Eij)² / Eij df = (I − 1)(J − 1)

It is 0 exactly when the observed table equals the independence table and grows with the distance from it. Under H0 (independence in the population) and with large expected counts it follows approximately a chi-square distribution. The degrees of freedom count the free parameters: a table with fixed N has IJ − 1 free cell probabilities, the independence model has (I − 1) + (J − 1), and the difference is (I − 1)(J − 1). The p-value is P(χ²df ≥ observed value).

Computing the p-value. With an integer number of degrees of freedom k the survival function of the chi-square distribution has a closed form, obtained by integrating its density by parts:

Q(x; 2) = e−x/2 Q(x; 1) = 2 (1 − Φ(√x)) Q(x; k + 2) = Q(x; k) + (x/2)k/2 e−x/2 / Γ(k/2 + 1)

So for even k the p-value is a finite sum of Poisson-like terms, and for odd k it is the normal tail plus a finite sum. For the normal tail I reuse the method of HW5 (power series below 2, Laplace's continued fraction above), which keeps full relative accuracy even for p-values like 10−22. The terms are formed through logarithms so that nothing overflows for large statistics. To check the code independently I wrote a short Python script with a different algorithm, the regularized incomplete gamma function Q(k/2, x/2) by power series and continued fraction with math.lgamma; on every table of this page and on a set of test points the two agree to at least 12 significant digits (relative difference below 10−12, even at p ≈ 10−229), and both return 0.05 at the textbook critical values 3.841 (1 df) and 5.991 (2 df).

For Gender × Admit, χ² = 92.21 with 1 degree of freedom, p ≈ 7.8 · 10−22. The likelihood-ratio statistic G² and the Yates-corrected χ² (91.61, the value that R's chisq.test reports by default for 2 × 2 tables) lead to the same conclusion. The odds ratio of 0.54 says that a woman's odds of admission were about half a man's. Cramér's V = 0.14 is a reminder that a tiny p-value is not a strong association: with N = 4526 even a moderate difference is far beyond sampling noise.

Check without the chi-square approximation: a permutation test

If X and Y are independent, the Y labels carry no information about X, so shuffling the Y column among the records produces tables that are just as likely as the observed one. Shuffling keeps both margins, hence E, fixed. Repeating it B times gives the distribution of χ² under H0 directly from the data, and the permutation p-value is (number of shuffled χ² ≥ observed + 1) / (B + 1).

The histogram of the shuffled tables follows the chi-square curve closely, which is the asymptotic theory working. For Gender × Admit the observed 92.2 is far beyond every shuffled value (the largest of 2000 was 13.2), so the permutation test can only say p < 1/2001; the chi-square formula is what gives the actual order of magnitude. Choose a single department in section 01 to see a case where the observed value falls inside the histogram. With a few hundred applicants and fixed margins, χ² can only take a limited set of values, so the histogram has gaps and the two p-values differ slightly (department E: 0.37 by permutation, 0.32 from the chi-square curve); both lead to the same decision.

05Independence in probability and in statistics

In probability independence is a property of a model. Two events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B); when P(B) > 0 this is the same as P(A | B) = P(A), knowing that B happened does not change the probability of A. Two random variables are independent when every event defined by X is independent of every event defined by Y; for discrete variables this reduces to

P(X = xi, Y = yj) = P(X = xi) · P(Y = yj) for every i, j equivalently P(Y = yj | X = xi) = P(Y = yj) for every i with P(X = xi) > 0

(in general, the joint distribution function is the product of the marginal ones, FXY(x, y) = FX(x) FY(y)). It is an exact statement about probabilities that are known or assumed, and it is symmetric: X independent of Y is the same as Y independent of X. Independence implies zero covariance but not the converse; for two binary variables, as in Gender × Admit, the two notions do coincide.

In statistics the same definition is applied to observed frequencies. Descriptively, X and Y are independent in a dataset when fij = fi· f·j for every cell, that is, when all row profiles are identical (and equal to the marginal of Y) or, equivalently, all column profiles are identical. Then nij = Eij in every cell and χ² = 0. In real data this practically never happens exactly, if only because ni· n·j / N is usually not an integer. So the statistical question is inferential: the records are a sample from a population with unknown probabilities πij, and we test

H0: πij = πi· π·j for all i, j against H1: not H0

The observed frequencies estimate the π's, E is the best estimate of the counts under H0, and χ² measures whether the gap between the two is larger than random sampling would produce. Three consequences are worth keeping in mind. A large p-value is not a proof of independence, only an absence of evidence against it. A small p-value shows that there is an association, not how strong it is; for that I report Cramér's V, which lies between 0 (independence in the data) and 1. And the conclusion concerns the population the data come from: here, the 1973 applicants to these six departments.

06Interpretation: one department at a time

The pooled table says that admission and gender are dependent, with men admitted more often. Before reading that as discrimination, look at the other two pairs of variables (selectable in section 01):

  • Dept × Admit is strongly dependent: χ² = 778.9 with 5 df, V = 0.41. Departments A and B admitted about 64% and 63% of their applicants, F only 6.4%.
  • Gender × Dept is even more strongly dependent: χ² = 1068.4 with 5 df, V = 0.49. Men applied mostly to A and B (51.5% of the men, against 7.2% of the women), women mostly to C, D, E and F, the departments with low admission rates.

So the department is associated with both variables, and it can produce an association between them by itself. The right check is to test Gender × Admit inside each department, where the department is held fixed:

Admission rate of women and men in each department and in the pooled data. Marker area grows with the number of applicants (the pooled markers have a fixed size).

In four departments out of six women were admitted more often than men, and in five of them (B to F) the difference is small and not significant: p-values between 0.32 and 0.61. Only department A shows a significant difference, χ² = 17.25, p = 3.3 · 10−5, and it goes the other way: 82.4% of the women against 62.1% of the men were admitted. The pooled gap of 14 percentage points against women appears because women applied mostly to departments that reject most applicants, regardless of gender. This reversal of an association after splitting by a third variable is known as Simpson's paradox.

Since the six departments are separate samples, the six statistics (1 df each) can be added into a test of conditional independence of gender and admission given the department: the sum is 19.94 with 6 df, p = 0.0028, and it comes almost entirely from department A; without A the sum is 2.69 with 5 df, p = 0.75. My conclusion for this dataset: gender and admission are statistically dependent overall; given the department, the data show no evidence of dependence in departments B to F, and a dependence in favour of women in department A.

07Association is not causation

The chi-square test detects statistical association: the distribution of one variable changes with the value of the other. It says nothing about why, and the statistic is symmetric in X and Y, so it cannot even tell which variable would be the cause. A dependence in a table of observational data can come from several different mechanisms:

  • a causal effect of X on Y (or of Y on X);
  • a common cause or confounder Z that influences both, as the department does here: an association between gender and admission appears in the pooled data with no gender effect inside the departments;
  • selection: the records only include who applied, and who decides to apply where is itself not random;
  • chance, which is the only alternative that the p-value controls.

The Berkeley case also shows that conditioning does not settle the causal question by itself. Splitting by department is the right move if the department is regarded as a confounder, a choice made by the applicant before the admission decision. If instead one asks why women applied to the more crowded departments, the department becomes part of the path from gender to the outcome, and the pooled difference is relevant again. The data alone cannot decide between these readings; the decision comes from knowledge of how the data were generated. Even inside a department, the similar admission rates do not prove the absence of any effect, since characteristics that were not recorded (grades, references, the specific programme) could differ between the two groups.

A causal claim needs either a design that breaks the link with every other variable, typically randomisation, or an explicit causal model together with data on the relevant confounders. What the chi-square test can establish is narrower and still useful: whether the observed association is too large to be explained by sampling variation, and where in the table it is located.
