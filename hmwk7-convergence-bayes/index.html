Modes of convergence, the √n rate, and Bayesian updating

The first part defines the four main modes of convergence of random variables, shows how they are related with two counterexamples, and follows each of them through the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem, including why the factor √n keeps appearing. The second part goes from conditional probability to Bayes' theorem and studies how prior and likelihood combine into the posterior, with a diagnostic test, a Beta-Binomial model on real admission data, and an exact check of how Bayesian intervals behave in repeated sampling.

Part 1 · Modes of convergence

01Four ways for a sequence to approach a limit

A random variable is a function X(ω) on a probability space (Ω, ℱ, P). For a sequence X1, X2, … "Xn → X" can mean several different things, depending on whether we look at single outcomes ω, at probabilities of large errors, at average errors, or only at distributions.

almost surely (a.s.) P( { ω : Xn(ω) → X(ω) } ) = 1 in probability for every ε > 0: P( |Xn − X| > ε ) → 0 in mean square (L²) E[ (Xn − X)² ] → 0 (in Lr: E|Xn − X|r → 0) in distribution Fn(x) = P(Xn ≤ x) → F(x) = P(X ≤ x) at every continuity point x of F
  • Almost sure convergence is ordinary convergence of numbers, path by path: apart from a set of outcomes of probability zero, the sequence Xn(ω) converges. An equivalent form, useful for simulations, is P(supm≥n |Xm − X| > ε) → 0 for every ε: the probability that the path will ever again leave the ε-band after time n goes to zero.
  • In probability only looks at one time n at a time: a large error at time n becomes unlikely, but nothing prevents each path from having large errors at infinitely many (increasingly rare) times.
  • In mean square controls the average size of the squared error. It is the natural mode when we work with variances, and it is what the mean squared error of an estimator measures. It is sensitive to rare but huge errors, which the first two modes ignore.
  • In distribution (weak convergence) only compares the laws: Xn and X need not even be defined on the same space. An equivalent definition is E g(Xn) → E g(X) for every bounded continuous g. The restriction to continuity points matters: Xn = 1/n converges in distribution to 0, although Fn(0) = 0 for every n while F(0) = 1.

02How the modes are related

Solid arrows always hold. Dashed arrows hold only under the condition written next to them.

  • a.s. ⇒ in probability, from the sup form above: the probability of an error at time n is at most the probability of an error at some time m ≥ n.
  • L² ⇒ in probability, by Markov's inequality applied to (Xn − X)²: P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E(Xn − X)² / ε². More generally Ls ⇒ Lr for s > r ≥ 1.
  • in probability ⇒ in distribution, and the converse holds when the limit is a constant c: then P(|Xn − c| > ε) ≤ Fn(c − ε) + 1 − Fn(c + ε/2), and both points are continuity points of the step function F, where F equals 0 and 1.
  • Convergence in probability gives a.s. convergence along a subsequence (Riesz). It gives L² convergence if the Xn are dominated, |Xn − X| ≤ Y with E Y² < ∞ (dominated convergence; uniform integrability is the general condition).

No other arrow holds. In distribution does not imply in probability: if X ~ N(0, 1) and Xn = −X, every Xn has the same law as X, but |Xn − X| = 2|X| does not shrink. The two sequences below show that a.s. and L² convergence do not imply each other.

(a) X1, X2, … independent, Xn ~ Bernoulli(1/n) (b) Xn = n · 1{U < 1/n}, one U ~ Uniform(0, 1) per path

In (a), P(Xn ≠ 0) = E Xn² = 1/n → 0, so Xn → 0 in probability and in L². But the events {Xn = 1} are independent and Σ 1/n = ∞, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma Xn = 1 for infinitely many n with probability 1: almost no path converges. Exactly, P(no one in [n, N]) = ∏m=n..N(1 − 1/m) = (n − 1)/N, which tends to 0 as N grows. In (b), every path with U > 0 is zero from n > 1/U on, so Xn → 0 almost surely, while E Xn² = n²·(1/n) = n → ∞ (even E Xn = 1 for all n, so there is no L¹ convergence either).

03The Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

Let X1, X2, … be independent and identically distributed (iid) with mean μ and variance σ², and X̄n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n. Each mode of convergence gives a different version of "the sample mean approaches μ", and the CLT adds the shape of the error.

  • L² law. By independence E(X̄n − μ)² = Var X̄n = σ²/n → 0. This needs only a finite variance and pairwise uncorrelated terms.
  • Weak law (in probability). Chebyshev's inequality turns the L² law into P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). Khinchin's theorem gives the same conclusion for iid variables with only E|X| < ∞. In statistics this is consistency of an estimator: θn → θ in probability.
  • Strong law (almost surely). Kolmogorov: for iid variables X̄n converges almost surely to a finite limit if and only if E|X| < ∞, and the limit is μ. This is what justifies reading a probability as a long-run frequency along a single sequence of trials, and Monte Carlo estimation from one long simulation. The Glivenko–Cantelli theorem extends it to the whole empirical distribution function: supx |Fn(x) − F(x)| → 0 almost surely.
  • CLT (in distribution). Lindeberg–Lévy: if 0 < σ² < ∞, Zn = √n (X̄n − μ)/σ → N(0, 1) in distribution. Only in distribution: the Zn of one path never settle down. For n ≤ m, Cov(Zn, Zm) = √(n/m), so E(Zm − Zn)² = 2 − 2√(n/m) does not shrink when m = 2n, 5n, …, which rules out convergence in probability.

With Exponential(1) draws and ε = 0.1, the share of paths outside the band at time n falls from 0.31 at n = 100 to 0.002 at n = 1000, always below the Chebyshev bound (which is crude: 1 at n = 100). The a.s. column is larger, as it must be, since it counts paths that leave the band at any later time; it also goes to 0. The simulated mean squared error matches σ²/n. Two distributions show where the hypotheses matter:

  • Pareto with α = 1.5 has a finite mean (3) but infinite variance. The strong law still holds, but slowly: at n = 5000 about 64% of the paths are still more than 0.1 away from 3. Without a finite variance there is no σ²/n and no √n: X̄n − μ shrinks like n1/α − 1 = n−1/3, with a skewed stable limit instead of a normal one.
  • Cauchy has no mean at all. X̄n has exactly the same Cauchy(0, 1) law for every n, so averaging does not help: P(|X̄n| > 0.1) = 1 − (2/π) arctan 0.1 ≈ 0.937 for all n, which is what the table shows.

The histogram uses the 2000 paths of the simulation above at the chosen n. For the skewed exponential the approach to N(0, 1) is visible between n = 1 and n = 30; for Bernoulli(0.3) the standardized mean is discrete and the distance to Φ falls more slowly. The Berry–Esseen theorem quantifies this: the distance is at most C · E|X − μ|³ / (σ³√n) with C < 0.48, again a √n rate. The last lines check the covariance formula for Zn and Zm on the same paths.

Other results built on these modes. Slutsky's theorem: if Xn → X in distribution and Yn → c in probability, then Xn + Yn → X + c and XnYn → cX in distribution. Since the sample standard deviation Sn → σ in probability (weak law applied to X²), √n (X̄n − μ)/Sn → N(0, 1): this is why large-sample confidence intervals and z-tests can use the estimated σ. With the continuous mapping theorem it also gives the delta method, √n (g(X̄n) − g(μ)) → N(0, g'(μ)²σ²), and under regularity conditions the maximum likelihood estimator satisfies √n (θn − θ) → N(0, 1/I(θ)), where I is the Fisher information. In short: the LLN modes say where an estimator goes, the CLT says how its error is distributed on the way, and that is what inference needs.

04Why √n

For any random variables with finite variances, the variance of a sum is the sum of all the variances and covariances:

Var(X1 + … + Xn) = Σi Var Xi + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj)

If the terms are independent, or just pairwise uncorrelated, all covariances vanish and variance is additive: Var Sn = nσ². Standard deviations are not additive; they add "in quadrature", so SD(Sn) = σ√n and SD(X̄n) = σ/√n. The errors of independent terms partly cancel: the sum grows like n but its fluctuation only like √n. This fixes the only scaling that gives a non-trivial limit:

Var( na (X̄n − μ) ) = σ² n2a − 1 → 0 if a < ½, σ² if a = ½, ∞ if a > ½

With a < ½ the error vanishes (the LLN, in L²), with a > ½ it explodes, and only a = ½ keeps it of order one, where the CLT tells its shape. The same √n is everywhere in inference: the standard error σ/√n, the confidence interval X̄ ± 1.96σ/√n, the sample size n = (zσ/E)² needed for a margin E, the 1/√N error of a Monte Carlo estimate, the asymptotic normality of the MLE and, in the Bayesian part below, the width of the posterior. One practical consequence is that halving an error costs four times the data.

Independence (or weak dependence) is what makes the rate possible. The chart compares the spread of X̄n for four sequences with the same marginal scale:

Dots: spread of the 2000 simulated means, measured as IQR/1.349 (equal to the standard deviation for normal data, and still defined for Cauchy). Dashed lines: exact values.

  • Independent N(0, 1): slope −½ on the log-log scale, the √n rate.
  • AR(1) with ρ = 0.8, Xt = 0.8 Xt−1 + εt: Cov(Xi, Xj) = ρ|i−j| decays fast, the covariance sum converges, and n Var X̄n → (1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ) = 9. The slope is still −½, but the spread is 3 times larger: n correlated observations are worth about n/9 independent ones.
  • Equicorrelated with ρ = 0.1 (a common shock W shared by all terms): Var X̄n = ρ + (1 − ρ)/n → 0.1. The covariances do not decay, the spread stops at √0.1 ≈ 0.32 and there is no law of large numbers: averaging cannot remove what all observations have in common.
  • Independent Cauchy: independence alone is not enough, the variance must also be finite. The spread does not decrease at all.

Part 2 · Conditional probability and Bayesian inference

05Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

For events A and B with P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B): the sample space is reduced to B and probabilities are renormalised inside it. Rearranging gives the multiplication rule, and summing over a partition A1, …, Ak gives the law of total probability:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A) P(A) P(B) = Σi P(B | Ai) P(Ai) Bayes: P(Ai | B) = P(B | Ai) P(Ai) / Σj P(B | Aj) P(Aj)

Bayes' theorem is just these two rules combined, and its use is to invert a conditional probability: from P(effect | cause), which is often what we know, to P(cause | effect), which is what we want. The two are different numbers and confusing them is a classic error. In the Berkeley admissions data of HW6, P(Admitted | Female) = 557/1835 = 30.4%, while Bayes gives P(Female | Admitted) = 0.304 · 0.405 / 0.388 = 31.7%, which is exactly the column profile 557/1755 computed there.

In probability theory conditioning is an operation on a known model. In statistics the model is unknown and the question is what conditioning on the data can tell us about it. Frequentist methods treat the parameter θ as fixed and only use probabilities of data given θ: the likelihood L(θ) = p(x | θ), and p-values P(statistic at least as extreme | H0), which are not P(H0 | data). Bayesian inference treats θ as uncertain, gives it a probability distribution, and applies Bayes' theorem to the data actually observed.

Example 1: a positive diagnostic test

A disease has prevalence 1% (prior), a test detects it with probability 99% (sensitivity) and is negative for 95% of healthy people (specificity). These two numbers are the likelihoods of a positive result under each hypothesis. The posterior probability of disease after a positive test is only 16.7%: among the positives, the few true cases are outnumbered by false positives from the much larger healthy group. Ignoring the prior here is the base-rate fallacy. A second, independent positive test uses 16.7% as the new prior and gives 79.8%.

The same test, with the same likelihoods, gives very different conclusions depending on the prior: the curve shows P(D | +) for every prevalence.

06Prior, likelihood, posterior

For a parameter θ with prior density p(θ) and data x with model p(x | θ), Bayes' theorem reads

p(θ | x) = p(x | θ) p(θ) / p(x), p(x) = ∫ p(x | θ) p(θ) dθ posterior ∝ likelihood × prior
  • Prior p(θ): what is believed about θ before the data, from earlier studies, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague choice.
  • Likelihood L(θ) = p(x | θ), the probability of the observed data as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it need not integrate to 1). It is the only channel through which data enter, and it is shared with frequentist inference, where its maximum is the MLE.
  • Posterior p(θ | x): the updated distribution of θ. The normalising constant p(x), the marginal likelihood, does not depend on θ, so the shape of the posterior is simply prior times likelihood.

The updating is sequential: if x1 and x2 are conditionally independent given θ, then p(θ | x1, x2) ∝ p(x2 | θ) p(θ | x1), so today's posterior is tomorrow's prior and the order of the data does not matter. This is why updating is central to Bayesian inference: every conclusion (point estimate, interval, probability of a hypothesis, prediction) is read from one object, the posterior, which absorbs new data in a coherent way.

Example 2: an admission probability (Beta-Binomial)

In department B of the Berkeley data, 17 of the 25 women who applied were admitted. Model the admissions as Binomial(n, θ), so the likelihood is θ17(1 − θ)8. With a Beta(a, b) prior, p(θ) ∝ θa−1(1 − θ)b−1, the product has the same form, so the posterior is Beta(a + k, b + n − k) (the Beta family is conjugate to the binomial). The prior acts like a + b earlier observations, and the posterior mean is a weighted average:

E(θ | x) = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · a/(a + b) + (1 − w) · k/n, w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

With the uniform prior the posterior is just the normalised likelihood (the two curves coincide in the chart), Beta(18, 9): mean 0.667, 95% credible interval [0.482, 0.828], and P(θ > 0.5 | data) = 0.962, a direct probability statement about the parameter that a confidence interval cannot give. The sceptical prior Beta(20, 20), centred at 0.5 and worth 40 observations, pulls the mean down to 0.569. A prior built from the men's admission rate in the same department (353/560 = 0.63, given a weight of 100 observations) gives 0.640 and a narrower interval: an informative prior adds information, and that is a strength when the information is right and a bias when it is not.

Sequential updating

400 simulated trials with θ = 0.3, uniform prior, posterior recomputed after every observation. The band narrows like 1/√n: the posterior standard deviation times √n approaches √(θ(1 − θ)) = 0.458, the same constant as the frequentist standard error. This is the Bernstein–von Mises theorem: for large n and a prior that is positive and continuous around the true value, the posterior is approximately N(θMLE, 1/(n I(θ))), and the prior is forgotten.

07Influence of the prior, advantages and limitations

How much the prior matters depends on how much data there are. The table keeps the observed proportion 17/25 = 0.68 and multiplies the sample size by 10 and 100 (the larger samples are hypothetical, to isolate the effect of n). Each cell is the posterior mean with the 95% credible interval.

At n = 25 the four priors give means from 0.57 to 0.67 and intervals that differ a lot; at n = 2500 all of them are within 0.003 of each other and of the MLE. The weight w of the prior falls like (a + b)/n. With small samples, the choice of prior is part of the conclusion and has to be reported and justified; with large samples the likelihood dominates.

Example 3: Bayesian intervals in repeated sampling

A Bayesian 95% interval is a statement about θ given the data; a frequentist 95% interval is a procedure that covers the true θ in 95% of repeated samples. It is still fair to ask how often a credible interval covers. For a binomial sample this can be computed exactly: for each θ, add the probabilities of the outcomes k whose interval contains θ.

For n = 20 the textbook Wald interval p ± 1.96√(p(1 − p)/n) covers the true value only 87.6% of the time at θ = 0.1 and 84.7% on average over θ, falling to 63.9% inside [0.05, 0.95]; it relies on the CLT, which is poor for small n and θ near 0 or 1. The credible intervals from the uniform and Jeffreys priors stay close to 95% on average. Here a mild prior also improves the frequentist behaviour, because it keeps intervals away from the degenerate zero width that the Wald interval has at k = 0 and k = n. (The coverage curves are saw-toothed because k is discrete.)

Example 4: normal data with known variance

With x1, …, xn ~ N(θ, σ²) and prior θ ~ N(μ0, τ0²), the posterior is normal with precisions (inverse variances) adding up:

1/τn² = 1/τ0² + n/σ² μn = τn² ( μ0/τ0² + n x̄/σ² )

The posterior mean shrinks x̄ towards μ0, with weights given by how precise each source is, and the posterior standard deviation behaves like σ/√n once n/σ² dominates 1/τ0²: the additivity of information here is the Bayesian face of the additivity of variance in section 04.

Advantages

  • Results are probabilities about the quantities of interest (P(θ > 0.5 | data), P(disease | positive)), which is usually what the question asks.
  • Prior information, from earlier studies or from related groups, can be used explicitly, which matters most when data are few.
  • Updating is coherent and sequential, and the same posterior serves for estimation, intervals, tests and predictions.
  • Priors regularise: they keep estimates away from impossible or degenerate values, as in the small-sample coverage example.

Limitations

  • The prior is a choice and, with small samples, it changes the conclusion; different analysts can reach different answers from the same data. Sensitivity analysis over several priors, as in the table above, is the honest response.
  • A prior that gives zero probability to a value can never be corrected by data, since the posterior is prior times likelihood (Cromwell's rule). Overconfident priors are almost as harmful.
  • "Non-informative" priors are not unique: uniform and Jeffreys priors differ, and a prior that is flat for θ is not flat for a transformation of θ.
  • Outside conjugate models the integral p(x) has no closed form and numerical methods (numerical integration, Markov chain Monte Carlo) are needed.
  • The posterior is only as good as the likelihood: if the model is wrong (here, applicants are certainly not identical coins), no prior fixes it. With large n the likelihood dominates the prior, for better or for worse.

08Implementation and checks

All simulations use a seeded generator (mulberry32), so every number on the page is reproducible; the text quotes the values of these seeds. Uniform draws are turned into the other distributions by inverting their CDFs, and normal draws use the Box–Muller transform. The normal CDF is the method of HW5.

For the Bayesian part I wrote log Γ (argument shifted above 20, then the Stirling series) and the regularized incomplete beta function Ix(a, b), the Beta CDF, with its continued fraction evaluated by the modified Lentz method; quantiles are found by bisection. To check them independently, a Python script recomputes the Beta CDF for integer parameters through the exact identity Ix(a, b) = P(Binomial(a + b − 1, x) ≥ a) with rational arithmetic, and for half-integer parameters by Simpson's rule; log Γ is compared with math.lgamma. All test values agree to at least 13 significant digits, and the script reproduces the Wald coverage 87.6% at n = 20, θ = 0.1.
